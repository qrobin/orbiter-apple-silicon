# Planetary maps

The Sun, Mercury, Venus atmosphere and surface, Moon, Mars, Saturn,
Uranus and Saturn-ring maps are by **Solar System Scope / INOVE**,
used under **Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0)**:
https://www.solarsystemscope.com/textures/ and
https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/. Earth uses the separately
credited NASA 8K image. Mercury, Venus surface, Moon and Mars use the
8192 × 4096 originals; the Saturn rings use the 8192 × 500 original. Sun,
Venus atmosphere and Saturn are 4096 × 2048, and Uranus is 2048 × 1024.
The source's files labelled 8K for the Sun,
Jupiter and Saturn actually have 4096 × 2048 pixels.
Solar System Scope notes that its maps contain enhanced color and invented
fill where observations are incomplete.

Jupiter uses NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center's 6000 × 3000 visible
surface map, shifted by half a turn to match the previous map's orientation:
https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/13007/. Its clouds are a snapshot, not live weather.

Neptune uses the 4096 × 2048 map by Askaniy Anpilogov, NASA/JPL-Caltech and
Björn Jónsson (including Karkoschka 1994), from Celestia Content, under
CC BY 3.0: https://github.com/CelestiaProject/CelestiaContent/blob/master/textures/hires/neptune.jpg
and https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/. Its clouds are a snapshot.

Europa uses the USGS Voyager/Galileo 500 m global mosaic, reduced to
8192 × 4096, shifted half a turn, and tinted. Missing pixels near the south
pole are filled from the previous map. The mosaic contains visible boundaries
between images and uneven resolution: https://astrogeology.usgs.gov/search/map/europa_voyager_galileo_ssi_global_mosaic_500m.

Ganymede uses the USGS Voyager/Galileo color mosaic, reduced to 8192 × 4096
and shifted in longitude. Small gaps near the poles use the previous map;
the original mosaic has uneven resolution and visible image joins:
https://astrogeology.usgs.gov/search/map/ganymede_voyager_galileo_ssi_color_global_mosaic_1_4km.

Io uses the USGS Voyager/Galileo color-merged mosaic, reduced to 8192 × 4096
and shifted slightly in longitude. It uses enhanced color and has uneven
coverage and image joins:
https://astrogeology.usgs.gov/search/map/io_galileo_ssi_voyager_color_merged_global_mosaic_1km.

Callisto uses the USGS Voyager/Galileo 1 km grayscale mosaic, reduced to
8192 × 4096, shifted half a turn, brightened and tinted. Gaps near the poles
use the previous map; coverage and lighting vary across the mosaic:
https://astrogeology.usgs.gov/search/map/callisto_voyager_galileo_ssi_global_mosaic_1km.

Phobos uses Philip Stooke's Cornell-controlled multi-mission photomosaic,
based on the Cornell mosaic by Peter Thomas and colleagues, from the NASA
PDS small-body maps archive. The 14400 × 7200 source is reduced to 8192 × 4096,
shifted slightly in longitude, and tinted. Deimos uses Stooke's Viking/Mars
Reconnaissance Orbiter mosaic at 7200 × 3600, tinted for display. The
underlying mosaic was compiled with Chris Jongkind and Megan Arntz and uses
control from Peter Thomas and colleagues:
https://sbnarchive.psi.edu/pds3/multi_mission/MULTI_SA_MULTI_6_STOOKEMAPS_V3_0/document/00_map_guide.html.

Enceladus uses a 7208 × 3604 crop of the Cassini mosaic PIA14937, with a
longitude shift and tint. It is contrast-enhanced and does not represent
uniform surface brightness. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/Space Science Institute;
https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/map-of-enceladus-december-2011/.

Mimas and Dione use 5760 × 2880 Cassini mosaics; Rhea uses a 2880 × 1440
Cassini/Voyager mosaic, and Tethys uses a Cassini mosaic reduced from
11520 × 5760 to 8192 × 4096. They are tinted and shifted in longitude; Dione is
cropped from the published map. Missing pixels in Mimas, Dione and Rhea use
the previous maps.
These contrast-enhanced mosaics contain uneven detail and brightness.
Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/Space Science Institute:
https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/map-of-mimas-june-2012/,
https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/map-of-dione-may-2008/,
https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/map-of-rhea-february-2010/ and
https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/map-of-tethys-june-2012/.

Iapetus uses the enhanced-color Cassini mosaic PIA18436, reduced to
8192 × 4096 and shifted in longitude. Its colors extend beyond visible light.
Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/Space Science Institute/Lunar and Planetary Institute;
https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/color-maps-of-iapetus-2014/.

Triton uses Paul Schenk's grid-free Voyager 2 color mosaic, reduced to
8192 × 4096. The unobserved northern area uses a blurred extrapolation of
nearby image colors and is not observed geography. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech,
Lunar and Planetary Institute and Paul Schenk:
https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Triton_map_no_grid.jpg.

Moon textures for Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, Oberon and Miranda derive from
NASA/JPL/Caltech/USGS Solar System Simulator
mosaics: https://maps.jpl.nasa.gov/tmaps/. Original mosaics are generally
1440 × 720, based on Voyager and in some cases Galileo imagery. They were
resampled to 2048 × 1024 and tinted for display. Large missing areas in some
maps use blended, transformed Phobos imagery as artistic filler; this does
not depict actual geography. Resampling does not add observed detail.

Titan uses the 5760 × 2880 Cassini ISS near-infrared surface mosaic PIA22770,
tinted for display. It represents surface brightness through the haze in
near-infrared, not a visible-light view. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/University
of Arizona; https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/titan-mosaic-the-surface-under-the-haze/.
Vesta uses the Dawn Framing Camera HAMO global mosaic, assembled at
8192 × 4096 from NASA/JPL Vesta Trek tiles:
https://trek.nasa.gov/tiles/apidoc/trekAPI.html?body=vesta.
Hyperion uses a resampled, tinted shaded-relief map by Philip Stooke from the
NASA PDS archive: https://sbnarchive.psi.edu/pds3/non_mission/EAR_A_3_RDR_STOOKEMAPS_V1_0/maps/.
Proteus and Nereid are fictional stand-ins made from transformed Phobos
imagery because complete maps were not available; their markings are not
observations of those moons.

All close-range solid-body materials add procedural detail. This detail and
the elevation geometry are visual approximations, not scientific data.
